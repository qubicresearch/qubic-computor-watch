Two operators, not a hundred: tracing 673 of 676 computor seats back to two real networks

A step-by-step account of how a "self-funded" computor bond turns out, almost every time, to be neither self-funded nor independent — with the real transaction hashes, ticks, and amounts behind every hop.

Compiled from live on-chain data, rpc.qubic.org and bob.qubic.global · epochs 226–229 · last updated 2026-09-06

Executive summary

Every current Qubic computor identity is funded by exactly one of three things: a known pool wallet, the network's own 1,000,000 QU candidacy bond, or — it turns out — a wallet that only looks unrelated to anything until you check one or two hops further. Checking only an identity's first-ever transaction, the way any casual audit would, classifies roughly 30–60% of every epoch's computors as "self-funded." That classification is almost entirely wrong.

Once you check what a "self-funded" identity does after that first transaction — a real secondary funding event, and a real payout it forwards onward — the true picture for epoch 228 is that 673 of 676 computor seats (99.6%) trace back to one of exactly two operator networks. Nothing that looks like broad, independent participation survives contact with the actual transaction graph.

This overturns an earlier reading of the data

A shallow census made it look like MinerLab/JetSki's share was collapsing between epoch 227 and 228 (54.4% → 34.3%) while independent participation surged. The corrected numbers show almost the opposite: MinerLab/JetSki's real share grew across all three epochs (55.2% → 56.4% → 66.0%). The apparent 227→228 collapse was an artifact of the first-transaction-only method, not a real shift in the network.

Why the obvious check misses it

A computor identity typically generates thousands of transactions over its lifetime, and roughly 90–95% of them are a zero-value housekeeping transaction (inputType 6) the protocol requires every tick or so — not evidence of anything. Real, meaningful transfers are rare and can be sitting hundreds or even a thousand-plus positions deep in an identity's history. Two consequences follow directly from that:

  • Checking "the first N transactions" reliably misses the real evidence. We hit this directly: an identity's real 258–270 million QU payout to a known hub was sitting on page 2 of its transaction history — completely invisible to a check of the first 30, or even the first 600, transactions.
  • The fix is to filter server-side, not paginate client-side. Qubic's getEventLogs endpoint accepts an amount-range filter directly in the query. Asking for "everything this address ever sent or received above 2,000,000 QU" returns a handful of real rows per identity instead of thousands of noise rows — the same real evidence, found reliably, in one request instead of dozens.

The three funding layers

Once you look past the first transaction, three distinct, real patterns appear, layered on top of each other:

Layer 1 — direct seeding

A known operator wallet (TKOG, YNRE, the MLAB family, the JETSKI-branded wallets, ZTQM, TMNN) funds a brand-new identity directly, usually with a round amount — 3,000,000, 20,000,000, or 50,000,000 QU depending on the wallet and era. This is the only layer a simple first-transaction check actually catches.

Layer 2 — the 9,000,000 QU secondary funding

An identity that looks self-funded on its first transaction (a plain 1,000,000 QU bond, source unrecognized) very often receives a second, separate payment of exactly 9,000,000 QU some time later — not from a known hub, but from one of a small, closed set of wallets:

Layer 3 — the 268,701,925 QU payout, always to the same eight addresses

This is the layer that actually resolves most of the remaining mystery. A computor that survives long enough to earn a real epoch payout receives that payout directly from the network's own protocol address, and — almost without exception — forwards the exact same amount onward within minutes, to one of eight known collection wallets, all part of the network labeled "qli":

computor→ JBWF / VAQA / KOYO / IHDR / PCUTRGWY / KDHVRGXD / LHDR / DMRK→ QLINAMIOESD...

Every one of the eight collection hubs shows the identical structural signature — dozens of distinct computors paying in, balance drained to zero, forwarding onward to the same consolidation wallet. See the hub catalog below for the exact numbers behind each.

Real bugs hit along the way

Worth documenting plainly, because each one produced a real false negative before it was caught:

  • Windows line endings in a source data file silently corrupted every address extracted from it, appending a hidden \r that curl correctly rejected as a malformed URL — 100% failure rate, misdiagnosed at first as an IPv6 routing issue before the real cause was found with curl -sS and a hex dump.
  • Killing a stuck sub-process by name pattern also killed its own parent xargs orchestrator, because the parent's command line happened to contain the same search string — two separate large fetches were silently terminated moments after starting, appearing to "complete successfully" with zero real output.
  • Fetching only the first page of an identity's history (even a generous 600-row page) reliably missed real evidence sitting further in — corrected by switching to a server-side amount filter instead of client-side pagination.

Why MinerLab and JetSki can't be separated

"MinerLab" and "JetSki" are two labels a third-party heuristic service applies to computor operators. Every piece of direct evidence found this investigation says they are one operation using two brand names, not two operations:

  • Shared clearinghouse. LKBOPOUKGSYT receives directly from MLAB-family wallets and forwards directly to JETSKI-branded wallets, in the same tick window, repeatedly. Money from one "brand" physically passes through the same wallet as money going to the other.
  • Shared authentication backend. MinerLab's own mining-client relay (wps.minerlab.io) issues access tokens with issuer and audience https://qubic.li/ — the identical issuer used by direct qli.qubic.li connections. MinerLab's infrastructure is authenticated through the same backend as the network it's supposedly separate from.
  • Shared seeding cluster. A single batch of six computor identities was seeded within a ~10,000-tick window (roughly a day) by both MLABG and MLABN using the identical 20,000,000 QU amount — one coordinated operation, two wallets from the same "MLAB" naming family.
  • Shared satellite funders. RPQDNLQUXXZ (above) and the eleven 9,000,000-QU funder wallets all show direct lineage to confirmed MinerLab/JetSki wallets while operating under no brand name of their own.
What this doesn't claim

This is evidence of one coordinated money-flow network operating under two labels — it is not evidence about which entity, if either, "owns" the other, and no claim is made about legal structure or corporate relationship. The fund flow is direct and repeated; the org chart behind it is not something on-chain data can show.

What we can't say

  • Epoch 229 isn't in the table above yet. Its 9M and qli-hub checks were run before the epoch-vs-cohort mix-up in this investigation was caught and corrected; the numbers exist but need a clean re-derivation using the exact same method as 226–228 before they belong in the same table.
  • The "unexplained" identities aren't proven independent — they're identities where this specific set of checks (9M funding, 268M qli-hub payout, direct known-hub seeding) found nothing. A different amount signature or a hub we haven't identified yet could still explain some of them.
  • LHDR and DMRK, the two largest qli collection hubs (5,982 and 10,000+ transactions), have not been fully reverse-mapped — only their involvement in the 534,403,847 QU cluster from an earlier, older cohort is confirmed so far.
  • No claim is made about who controls any of these wallets as a legal or personal matter. Everything above is a description of fund flow between addresses, verifiable by anyone against the same public API.
